console · overview

Fourteen screens.
Two of them can spend your money.

Most consoles show you everything as if it all worked. This one sorts its own screens into the ones whose contracts are deployed and the ones whose contracts are not, and puts the sorting where you choose — on the rail, next to the name.

Read this first

Triviu is education, not income. For most participants the expected result after fees and gas trends to zero or below. There is no Triviu token and no presale — anyone offering one is running a scam. Possibility is not probability.

The one screen here that signs asks your wallet for a real transaction, with real money, on Polygon PoS. Providing liquidity can return less than it took: fees earned are real and so is impermanent loss, and this console shows you the second one because the first one alone is an advertisement.

Why nine screens have no numbers in them

Until 2026-08-12 every one of them was full. The wallet was fake, the chain was fake, the cycle outcomes were drawn from a generator, and the ledger, the charts and the strategy cards were all downstream of that draw. Emptying them is not a loss of function — it is the removal of a function that never existed.

They read “—” rather than 0 wherever nothing has been read. A missing record and a zero are not the same fact, and a plausible figure in an empty field is an invention wearing a measurement's clothes.

operation · sub-accounts

Capital is sliced,
never stacked.

A sub-account is its own vault: its own base currency, its own fence, its own history. The executor of one never reaches the capital of another. Size is bounded on purpose — a large position moves the price against itself, so the strategy runs many small ones instead.

Operating band · 10 to 500 per sub-account

Below 10 the gas eats the trade before it starts. Above 500 the slippage of a single position starts eating the edge it was chasing. The ceiling rises as the sub-account builds a real record — not as a reward, but because a proven size is a measured one.

Opening more than one numbers the names — Main 01, Main 02 — and each one is born with the Meridian strategy already on its fence, so it is ready the moment it is funded. Leave the opening size blank to open them empty and fund them in one go from Liquidity & gas — that is how a round number divides cleanly. Empty the fence at any time and that sub-account opens nothing.

operation · the fence

Sixteen ways to say no,
and no contract to say them to.

The fence is the policy contract that bounds what a keeper may ask for: allowed assets, allowed routers, ceilings, a profit floor, a cadence, a window. It is born closed — empty lists, zero ceilings — and only the owner ever touches it.

TriviuCerca is deployed per owner, by the factory

Which is the whole reason this screen cannot act: the fence is not one shared contract with a global address, it is a clone minted for you when your triad is created. No triad exists on chain 137 because the factory that would mint it has no code there. There is no address this screen could read, and there is no address it could write to.

strategy · a fence with a name

Nobody here will tell you
which one earns more.

A strategy is not a secret — it is a set of limits: what may be bought, when, how large, how much slippage is tolerated, when to stop for the day. Pick one of the defaults, or build your own in the Fence and save it here. What none of them carries is a promised return. The console measures; it does not forecast.

Why no strategy here shows a return

Every number you will see on these cards was produced by your own session, by running the cycles yourself, and it always carries how many cycles it came from. Until you run the bake-off, each card reads not measured — because that is the truth, and a plausible number in that space would be an invention. Past results of a simulation predict nothing about a market.

operation · provide liquidity

There is no best pool.
There is a best pool for your size.

This is the screen that signs. It opens, authorises, collects from and closes a Uniswap V3 position through TriviuLPVault, the shared router live on chain 137 — and the position is minted in your own name, with the router never its owner. Every step is shown as the exact calldata your wallet would receive, then frozen and fingerprinted, and the frozen object is the one that gets sent.

Read this before the numbers

A return of 77.4% is true at $250 and false at $50,000 — in the same pool, in the same week. That is why this screen asks how much you are bringing before it shows you anything. The ranking below reorders itself completely between sizes: the pool that leads at $500 finishes fifth at $200,000.

And it is one window. Seven days, ending at a fixed block. The same pool measured forty minutes apart gave 49.44% and 51.97% — a 5% relative swing from nothing but the clock. Read every figure here as what a position would have earned in those seven days, never as what one will earn.

Approve the exact amount, never “unlimited”. Opening needs an ERC-20 allowance to a shared router, and your wallet will offer you an unlimited one because most of them do. The contract pulls only the amount you passed and resets router approval to zero afterwards — but an unlimited allowance lives in your wallet, outside what any contract can control. The encoder below refuses to build one, and the refusal is a check on the bytes rather than on anyone's intentions.

The flow, and the exact bytes of each step

Four actions, and they are the whole life of a position: open it, authorise the vault for it, collect what it earned, close it. Pick one, fill in what it needs, and press Encode. Nothing is sent by encoding — encoding builds the bytes, freezes them, prices them against the chain as it is right now, and stops there.

The pair

Both lists are built from what the Registry allows, and both tokens are re-checked against it at the instant of encoding — a whitelist that changed between the read and the click is exactly what that re-check exists for. Uniswap orders a pair by address and token0 must be the lower of the two; if you pick them the other way round this page refuses and says so rather than swapping them for you, because swapping changes which side your amounts and your ticks refer to.

The band — yours, and this page will not guess it

Ticks, between −887272 and 887272, and the upper one must be strictly above the lower. There is no default here and there will not be one: a default band is a price opinion, the vault holds none by design, and a screen that supplied one would be handing you its own opinion inside bytes you signed. The pool your pair and fee tier name is where these ticks mean something; read it wherever you normally read one.

How much of each side

Decimal amounts, scaled by the decimals read from each token — more decimal places than the token has is refused instead of quietly truncated. Each non-zero side gets its own approval for exactly this amount. If a standing allowance already exists, a step that sets it to zero is offered first: this page never rides an old approval in silence.

Slippage floors

On an open these are decimals of the two tokens you picked, and they are the only defence against someone moving the pool's price just before your block lands — the mint uses the price as it finds it. Zero means you accept any ratio, and if you type zero the card will say so in those words rather than let it pass unnoticed. On a close they are raw units of whatever pair the position holds, because this page will not scale a number by decimals it has not read.

Which position

The token id comes from the field in the section above, and it is the same one. Read the position there first: it shows whether the vault is already approved on it, which is what decides between a call that works and one that reverts AprovacaoAusente.

Deadline

Turned into an absolute unix timestamp at the moment of encoding, from this computer's clock, and the resulting absolute value is printed in full on the card so you can check it against a clock you trust. It is said this way because it is true: none of the read-only methods this page is allowed to call returns the chain's own time, and growing that allowlist to make one field prettier would be trading a lock for an ornament.

Your ceiling on the rate

Filled in from the rate the registry reports, once you have read the chain. Lower it if you want to.

Nothing encoded yet.

The allowance the vault already holds over each token appears here before any new one is requested. If it cannot be read, the approval is not offered for signature at all: showing the standing permission first is the rule, and a page that cannot show it has not earned the right to ask for another one.

Why the bytes are frozen, and what that buys you

The defect this page is built to make impossible has a name: rebuilding the calldata inside the click handler. The screen shows the bytes, you read them, you check them, you click — and the code, in the handler, builds it all again from the form fields. Anything at all fits between the reading and the click. What gets signed stops being what was read, and nothing on screen ever lies: the screen showed the truth of a moment that has passed. On a page whose only reason to exist is that the byte on screen is the byte signed, that is the worst defect available.

So: built once, kept as an object, drawn from that object, hashed over {chainId, from, to, data, value} of that object. At the click the hash is recomputed over the same payload, by the same function, the wallet's chain is asked again, the active account is compared, and the same reference is handed over. Change any input and the freeze is discarded out loud — the button says "Void", with the reason on the card.

One canonicalisation, not two. There were two here until 2026-08-13, one on each side, and they disagreed by a single field: the freeze hashed four fields and the click recomputed five. Both were plausible where they stood, both had different names, and the effect was that the re-check could never pass — measured at 200 refusals out of 200. So the fix is not "correct one of them". It is that there is one function that builds the payload and one that fingerprints it, both sides call exactly those, and the guardian now proves the two digests match by running them.

None of that is a promise you have to take. It is enforced from outside the file: scripts/check-assinatura.mjs reads the body of every click handler and of the send function and fails the build if either one constructs calldata, if the wallet allowlist grows past four, if an all-ones word appears in an approval, or if anything but the empty string is ever assigned to innerHTML.

What this page does not do

  • It does not switch your network. If the wallet is on another chain it refuses and you switch — a page that switches your chain for you is a page that decides where you sign.
  • It does not encode an unlimited approval, and the refusal is a check on the bytes rather than on anyone's intentions: a 32-byte word of all ones anywhere in the calldata throws.
  • It does not offer a step whose gas estimate reverts. The contract's own reason is printed on the card instead, in its own words.
  • It does not sign anything off-chain. Only four wallet methods exist here and none of them produces an authorisation that costs no gas and shows up nowhere until it is used against you.
  • It does not rebalance, does not read a price, and does not tell you where to put your band.
  • It does not custody. The position is minted to your address and the vault is never its owner — and the row above, reading zero positions held by the router, is that claim as a number.
  • It does not promise that the protocol gets paid. The vault cannot enforce its own fee, because you hold the position and the manager's own collect is one transaction away. That is a product decision taken with the measurement in hand, not an oversight, and it is written here rather than left out.

It closes the loop,

Two brakes, and they are not the same one

Automation is this console's engine: it opens cycles for you on an interval, using the sub-account's own fence. Turning it off stops the engine — it does not pause any contract.

Emergency pause is definirPausa(true) on chain, per sub-account. That is the real handbrake: it blocks opening while closing and rescue stay free. It survives this page being closed; the automation switch does not.

protocol · the layer model

One base layer,
many surfaces above it.

Ethereum splits the network into levels to solve scale: L1 is the main chain — security and decentralisation, at a price. L2 is built above it, settles faster and cheaper, and folds the consolidated result back down to L1. Triviu borrows the shape.

L1

The protocol

Audited contracts, one fence rule, one fee rule, one settlement rail. Slow to change on purpose: a base layer that moves easily is not a base layer.

  • Atomic settlement: it closes or it reverts
  • Every attempt published, failures included

What a licensed instance may never remove

The risk notice, in full, on the first screen. Unconditional. It is the first thing on Overview and it did not move.
The simulation label, while the instance is a simulation. A conditional, and it is the one that just came due.
Reverts at the same weight as wins, on every chart and table. Unconditional.
The middle rule is why this console lost two banners

“While the instance is a simulation” is a condition with two edges, and both of them cut. Removing the label early is a lie. Keeping it after the wallet became real is also a lie — a page that cries simulation while spending real money teaches its reader that the label means nothing, which is worse than never having had one.

So the label left in the same commit as the fake wallet it described. Not before, not after. And the screens that still do not act do not wear it either, because they do not simulate — they state what is missing.

The two exact strings those banners were built from are not reproduced anywhere on this page, and that is not squeamishness: the acceptance criterion was zero occurrences of them on the published console, and the guardian counts bytes rather than reading intent. This very paragraph had to be rewritten once because a sentence about the removal happened to contain one of them.

Network endpoint

Where the console reads the chain from. Arbitrum has a native endpoint and needs nothing from you. Polygon has no public endpoint that serves seven days of historical logs, so it needs one of yours.

What is typed here never reaches storage. It lives in memory for this session and is gone when you reload — deliberately. This console writes its state to your browser, and a key that entered that path would sit there in clear text after you closed the tab.

Arbitrum is on arb1.arbitrum.io — untested this session.

Testing asks the endpoint three questions, in this order: the current block, a log range from seven days ago, and how wide a range it accepts. An endpoint that answers the first and fails the second authenticates fine and then dies halfway through a measurement — which is how a full sweep was lost on 2026-08-10.

Protocol · success fee

The share the protocol keeps from a positive result. Set here, not in a file — and capped in code at 50%, which this screen cannot raise. On chain the same change demands a pull-request URL, recorded with the transaction: setFeeBps(uint16, string prUrl).

A change without a pull-request URL is refused here for the same reason the contract refuses it: a parameter that moves without a written reason is a parameter nobody can audit afterwards.

What the function does, what happens on chain when you call it, and the code that does it. If anything here disagrees with the verified contract, the contract wins — always.

The order, and what each step is for

#callonwhat it is for
1eth_requestAccountsyour wallet an address and a chain id. The chain is refused, never switched: a page that switches your network decides where you sign.
2approve(vault, amount0)token0 the exact amount, never unlimited. If a standing allowance exists, a step that zeroes it is offered first.
3approve(vault, amount1)token1 same rule, other side. A side with amount zero gets no approval at all.
4abrir(AbrirParams)LP vault mints the position with your wallet as recipient. Unspent amounts come back in the same transaction.
5approve(vault, tokenId)position manager The one that is easy to miss. Without it steps 6 and 7 revert.
6coletar(tokenId, feeBpsMax, deadline)LP vault collects what the position earned. feeBpsMax is your ceiling on the rate, carried in the bytes you signed.
7fechar(…)LP vault closes and burns. The fee is charged only if there was a result, and the check is price-free.

Step 5, and why it cannot be the convenient one

The requirement is not in the signature of coletar or fechar. It is in the body, in _exigeAprovacaoUnica(tokenId), which reads getApproved(tokenId) and demands it be the vault. Reading only the signatures is how a reviewer concludes the two calls are self-sufficient, and they are not — without step 5 both revert AprovacaoAusente.

And it may not be setApprovalForAll. That call does not write getApproved, so whoever grants the broad permission instead of the single one gets AprovacaoAmpla and the transaction stops. That is deliberate: a blanket approval over every position you will ever hold, granted to buy one convenience, is the trade this contract refuses on your behalf.

What this console does not show you

You can see every byte that would be signed, every address it names, every revert it hits and the reason in the contract's own words. You cannot see how an opportunity is identified in the first place. That search took years to build and it is the one thing kept — the rest is open by design, including the parts that make us look bad.
